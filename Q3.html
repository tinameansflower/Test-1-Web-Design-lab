Tim Berners Lee's Original Research: Physics vs. Uses of the World Wide Web

Tim Berners-Lee worked at CERN as a software engineer, worked in a particle physics laboratory and is the inventor of the World Wide Web (1989) The World Wide Web— originally designed at CERN was meant to solve a problem of incompatible computers, operating systems, and databases that made it difficult to share data with one another. The purpose for which he created HTML (to structure documents) and HTTP (for client-server communication) was to solve and create a platform to share physics research and technical documents among scientists, and that's what it was primarily used for from 1989 to 1994. It was an open-source platform allowing link sharing through URLs and an overall collaborative platform without gatekeeping, fees, or tracking mechanisms.

Currently, the purpose of the World Wide Web has shifted from mainly sharing scientific data/research for academic purposes to casual/general communication through streaming, social media (Instagram, Twitter, Reddit, etc), emailing, etc. Additionally, it has grown through online shopping, in other words, e-commerce. The web still contains various informative and academic information, but has significantly grown in the aspect of buying and selling goods worldwide along with communicating across the globe to other people. There are various ads and subscriptions along with fees that go along with it. Of course, there are still educational uses but mainly shifted onto entertainment and streaming. Today, the World Wide Web also contributes to remote work and collaboration so the original purpose still stands.

Overall, the WWW advanced greatly, originally meant to solve one issue: the sharing of information and academic research, shifting from not only being a source of shared information to the addition of e-commerce, a general communication program, entertainment purposes, and even changing the free, open-source info into a money-making opportunity through the addition of ads and subscriptions.
